Far from Earth, a health reading changes. Is it a warning, a passing fluctuation, or a sensor issue? Our challenge: help astronauts understand their health on long missions, when support may be delayed. We built PROHOR: a research prototype that turns scattered readings into context, compares them with personal history, and helps a human decide what to review next. We are team Skorpe Monad. Tufayel Ahmed, our team lead, main developer and designer. Annapurna Das Protyasha, researcher and concept maker. Rakthim Deb Rudro, advisor. Mir Shahi Ahmod, data scientist. And Tonusri Deb Diggi, video and photo editor. The idea is simple. First, gather readings. Next, compare each reading with prior days, using a transparent personal baseline. Then prepare a routine follow-up and a handover. PROHOR gives context; it does not diagnose illness. Here is one simulated example. Today’s sleep is 5.6 hours; the person’s previous median is 7.4. PROHOR uses the median and interquartile range of prior days to flag a change. Its band is an unvalidated prototype rule, not a medical threshold. The health atlas makes the information easier to explore. Choose a body system to see its available indicators and context. A color points to a reading that needs review, or limited data. It does not diagnose an organ. PROHOR uses NASA’s Open Science Data Repository, OSD-575, in its research explorer. The Inspiration4 blood serum metabolic panel supplies real measurements behind these charts, with original values, units, and source rows. This is historical research, separate from our simulated crew, and does not validate its sleep flags. NOAA’s planetary Kp adds Earth geomagnetic context. Cabin readings are separate. Neither proves the cause of a health change, or represents a crew member’s personal radiation dose. Here is a real import in the prototype. A sample CSV brings readings into the local browser. Required fields and units are checked, and an invalid row rejects the import. Local inputs remain separate from NASA research and the simulated mission. After the first successful load and cache, the workspace can work offline. We reloaded it without a network. Records stay in the browser, with no hidden synchronization. This browser storage is not a secure clinical record system. A flag is a starting point, not a decision. The care plan offers routine follow-up, such as protecting a sleep window. A structured handover brings the reading and crew notes together for review. It can be exported or printed; nothing is sent automatically. Why does this matter? Our goal is not to replace doctors. It is to help a crew notice a change, understand the evidence, and bring a clearer story to a human reviewer, even when a connection is unavailable. On longer missions, this transparent, offline-capable workflow could make information easier to review when support is far away. A number alone is not a story. PROHOR brings the change, the person’s baseline, and the evidence together, so a crew can prepare for human review, even when a connection is unavailable. Our next step is to test this research prototype with clinicians and mission operators. It is not a medical device. We are Skorpe Monad. PROHOR: a quiet watch for a long journey.
